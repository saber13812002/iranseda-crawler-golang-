Every social action involves two levels of rights: divine rights, which are actually personal rights regarding individual spiritual growth and fall within the realm of voluntary choice, and human rights, which pertain to social relations and external obligations. In the private sphere, no one has the right to interfere with an individual's beliefs or practices, such as prayer or fasting, because faith and worship are meaningless without freedom and awareness. However, when an individual enters the public sphere, their behavior is no longer purely personal and becomes subject to law and the rights of others. The fundamental difference from the liberal approach is that while liberals cannot mandate dress codes, in a religious society, preserving public sanctity and preventing social corruption based on Islamic principles is both possible and necessary. Therefore, enforcing dress codes in public spaces is not a religious coercion but a protection of collective rights and social order. Sometimes, seemingly theoretical objections are rooted in emotions or practical issues and cannot be resolved by pure argumentation alone, requiring a deeper investigation into their underlying causes. Ultimately, the boundary between private and public spheres determines when ethics prevail and when the law applies.
