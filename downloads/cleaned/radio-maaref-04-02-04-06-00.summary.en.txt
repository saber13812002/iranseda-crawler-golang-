This program examines the principle of "the obligation to prevent probable harm" in Islamic theology, analyzing its historical development and application within the Mu'tazilite school. The speaker explains that although Mu'tazilite theologians did not treat this rule as an independent creedal issue, they utilized it to prove concepts such as the obligation of rational inquiry, the necessity of an Imam, and the duty of repentance. By reviewing the views of early Mu'tazilites who considered knowledge of God to be innate, it is suggested that reliance on this principle likely began in the late second or early third century AH. In the context of Imamate, the Mu'tazilites argued for the rational necessity of appointing a just ruler to prevent societal chaos, contrasting this view with the Kharijites. Furthermore, in discussions regarding enjoining good and forbidding evil, some Mu'tazilites like Abu Hashim al-Jubba'i attributed the obligation of this act to reason when it prevented personal harm. Finally, the program compares the Ash'arite view, which considers the principle a matter of consensus, with the Mu'tazilite perspective that regards it as rational, emphasizing the rule's role in preventing social corruption.
