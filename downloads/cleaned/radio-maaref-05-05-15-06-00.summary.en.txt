This session of the Kalam program focused on the rule of "repelling probable harm" and the required degree of probability for its rational obligation.
The first theory, which considered any absolute probability sufficient, was questioned due to weak support from reason and narrations.
The second theory, adopted by most Shia jurists and theologians, holds that "strong probability" (Zann) substitutes for certainty and necessitates the obligation to repel harm.
The primary reason for this view is that most worldly and otherworldly harms are inherently probable; limiting the rule to certainty would render it practically useless.
However, some contemporary scholars like Wahid Khorasani argued that the criterion is not solely "Zann," but also the "significance of the harm."
According to this perspective, even if the probability is less than Zann, reason mandates caution and repelling the harm if it is highly significant.
Conversely, if the harm is minor, reason may not impose an obligation even with strong probability, considering it merely recommended.
The final conclusion is that the rational ruling on repelling harm depends on a combination of the degree of probability (certainty or strong probability) and the severity of the harm.
