This theological program discusses the basis of the principle "obligation to repel probable harm" and the role of consensus in Islamic theology.
The initial view that consensus is the primary basis for this principle was rejected because consensus is not an independent source of law but rather reveals underlying proofs from the Quran, Hadith, or reason.
The famous rational argument for the validity of consensus suggests that unanimous agreement implies a definitive proof, but this was refuted by the possibility of collective error.
While unanimity rules out collective lying, it does not eliminate the possibility of a shared mistake based on weak reasoning, as large groups can still hold incorrect beliefs.
Consequently, the second and more dominant view holds that reason is the true basis for this principle, a position supported by most theologians.
The Mu'tazilites and Imamiyya utilized this principle to prove the obligation of rational inquiry into religion and the necessity of Imamate.
They argued that the absence of an Imam or rational thought leads to chaos and harm, and since repelling harm is rationally obligatory, these duties are also mandatory.
Ultimately, this principle serves as a fundamental rational axiom in theological and jurisprudential debates, particularly regarding the Imamate and rational obligations.
