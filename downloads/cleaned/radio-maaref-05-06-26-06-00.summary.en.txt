This segment of the program continues the theological and jurisprudential discussion on the principle of "repelling probable harm." Three main claims are presented, starting with the assertion that the obligation to repel harm is not limited to certainty or strong probability but extends to lower degrees of doubt. The second claim states that probabilities below fifty percent are also covered by this rule, provided the potential harm is significant and the probability is considered substantial. The third claim emphasizes that the degree of the harm's importance directly influences the level of probability at which reason mandates its repulsion.
To substantiate these claims, three rational arguments are offered, including the verdict of conscience, the combination of doubt and probability, and the logical impossibility of proving an obligation against God. Quranic verses, such as the sixth verse of Surah Al-Mu'minun, are interpreted as textual evidence that warns audiences of potential divine punishment, urging them to take these possibilities seriously and act with caution.
Narrations from Imam Sadiq and Imam Reza (PBUH) are cited as supporting evidence, comparing faith in the face of doubt to following a doctor's advice for a critical illness. The core argument is that if God does not exist, the cost of faith is negligible, but if He does, the cost of disbelief is eternal and immense. Consequently, reason dictates that one must embrace faith and avoid sin to repel this potentially catastrophic harm.
