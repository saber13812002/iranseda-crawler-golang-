This segment of the program discusses the principle of "avoiding suspected harm" as a self-evident rational axiom rather than a theoretical one. Theologians and jurists from the Imami and Mu'tazili schools consider this rule so fundamental that they do not provide independent arguments for it, relying instead on the consensus of practice and the rational condemnation of neglecting it. The most significant application of this principle is in proving the obligation of divine knowledge, as the possibility of God's existence and the afterlife creates a potential harm that reason dictates must be investigated to avoid potential punishment. In Usul al-Fiqh, this rule serves as the primary basis for precaution when evidence conflicts and reliance on consensus or emulation is not possible. Secondary arguments, such as the practice of the wise and the rational ugliness of preferring the less probable over the more probable, are merely indicators of the principle's self-evident nature rather than independent proofs. Finally, the subtle distinction between "suspected" harm (low probability) and "probable" harm (high probability) is examined, concluding that the rule remains binding even in cases of low probability due to the rational ugliness of neglecting caution.
