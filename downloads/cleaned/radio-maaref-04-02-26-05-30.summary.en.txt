This philosophical discussion examines and confirms the precise definition of "substance" as a nature whose external existence does not require a subject.
A key point is that while the nature of substance is inherently "subjectless," proving its actual existence in the external world is not self-evident and requires logical proof.
A subtle distinction is made between "primary" predication (which is essential and causeless) and "common" predication (which requires an external cause), placing the external existence of substance in the latter category.
Contrary to common belief, adding the qualifier "subjectless" to the concept of existence does not automatically make it a genus, as this qualifier is not essential.
It is also explained that substance in mental existence simultaneously possesses a substantial nature and an accidental existence, which does not create a contradiction.
This difference arises from the distinction in predication modes; the nature of substance is essentially self-subsisting, but its mental existence is subsisting in another (the mind).
Finally, the compatibility between the essential nature and the accidental mental existence is resolved by distinguishing between nature and existence, as well as between primary and common predication.
