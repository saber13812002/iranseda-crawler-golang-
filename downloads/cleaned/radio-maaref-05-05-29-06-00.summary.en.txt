This radio program examined various jurisprudential perspectives on the rule of "repelling probable harm" and the required degree of probability for its obligation. The third viewpoint, articulated by Sayyid Taki Tabatabai and Ayatollah Sistani, posits that the primary criterion is the significance of the harm rather than the level of probability. According to this view, if the harm is significant, even a weak probability (such as one percent) necessitates repelling it, whereas if the harm is trivial, even a strong probability does not create an obligation. Examples such as the prohibition of smoking at gas stations due to explosion risks, contrasted with consuming soda due to its minor harm, were used to illustrate this principle. Critics argue that reason does not mandate repelling harm in cases of extremely low probability, such as car accidents, and that attending to such possibilities is obsessive. Responses were also provided to critiques suggesting that this rule might invalidate religious laws, noting that most people act based on higher probabilities than those cited. The discussion was concluded by deferring the examination of the fourth viewpoint and further arguments to the next session.
