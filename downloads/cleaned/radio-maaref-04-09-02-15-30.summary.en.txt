In this radio program, Professors Jafari and Babaei emphasized that political jurisprudence is inherently embedded in the text of Islam from its inception. They argued that the political dimension of the prophets' movements was more prominent than individual matters, and that society remained active toward governmental objectives even during the Minor Occultation. Historical analysis reveals that early jurists were forced to use alternative terms like "Sultan" instead of "Imamate" due to severe political restrictions. A notable example of this pressure is the burning of Sheikh Tusi's library in Baghdad by Sunni rulers, highlighting the high risks of expressing political views at that time. Shahid al-Awwal also took a significant step in governmental jurisprudence by compiling a treatise for soldiers. Finally, citing Kashif al-Ghita, establishing a government was defined as a mandatory duty for the jurist or even any believer in the absence of an absolute jurist. These discussions demonstrate that establishing a government is an inseparable part of Islam, rather than a secondary issue.
