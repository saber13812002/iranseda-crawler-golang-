This segment of the program examines the jurisprudential and theological basis of the rule "repelling probable harm," highlighting the perspectives of the Mu'tazila and Ash'ari schools.
The central point is the definition of "majority probability" (ahmali), which is not limited to a numerical percentage but is the product of the probability's magnitude and the significance of the potential harm.
Criteria for assessing the importance of harm include its duration (such as eternity in afterlife harms), the intensity of pain, the scope of impact (individual or societal), and whether the harm is irreversible.
The practical criterion for determining if a probability is significant is the generation of "fear" or anxiety in the general public; if a probability does not cause such fear, reason deems it negligible.
Prominent scholars like Sheikh Ansari and Ayatollah Khoei emphasize that fear is the primary indicator for establishing the necessity of repelling harm.
This rule plays a fundamental role not only in specific jurisprudential issues, such as performing tayammum instead of wudu in dangerous conditions, but also in the entirety of individual and social life.
Finally, citing Quranic verses and narrations, it is demonstrated that preventing harm is obligatory even when the probability is less than fifty percent, provided the potential harm is significant.
